Status note
The shared language

One design system, ten dialects

Every concept draws from the same warm editorial palette, type ramp and 8pt grid. What differs is grammar — how space is organised, what the primary object is, and where the eye is asked to go. That constraint is what makes the ten read as one studio's thinking rather than ten unrelated mockups.

01

Decision Graph

Built

Work is a living graph of decisions. Every drawing, option and approval is evidence attached to a node — and the graph, not a list, is the project.

Layout logic

A 72px icon rail, an unbounded graph canvas from x72–1000, and a 440px inspector docked right on bone. The graph is deliberately not auto-arranged into tidy columns — organic placement signals that this is a map of thought, not a pipeline.

The inspector is the only dense region on screen. Everything else is air.

Navigation philosophy

There is no back button and no folder tree. You navigate by zooming and following edges. The rail switches lens (Graph / Rooms / Objects / Ledger / Wall) over the same underlying data — never a different place, only a different reading.

Why it fits the model

Designers work by making a move and watching consequences propagate. A graph is the only structure that shows consequence. Reopening a node visibly threatens its descendants — the interface makes the cost of changing your mind legible for the first time.

Understood in five seconds

“These circles are things we decided. The red ones are in trouble. The red line is why.” No legend needed to feel it; the legend at the bottom only names what the eye already grouped.

02

Living Rooms

Built

The project isn't progressing — each room is. Rooms are organisms with vitals, maturity and health; they grow, stall, and sometimes regress.

Layout logic

A 280px plan-key column pinned left, then a 3×2 grid of 340×356 room cards on a 372/388 pitch. The plan is small and the rooms are large — a deliberate inversion of every architecture tool, which makes the drawing dominant. Here the drawing is only the key; the living thing is the room.

Navigation philosophy

Spatial, not hierarchical. You click a room in the plan or a card in the grid — same destination. There is no “projects → phases → tasks” ladder. Depth is achieved by entering a room, never by descending a tree.

Why it fits the model

FF&E schedules are already room-tabbed; payments are already room-scoped; presentations are already room-by-room. This concept simply stops fighting the industry's native container and makes it the primitive.

Understood in five seconds

“Every room has a pulse.” The arc reads as a life sign, the three vitals read as a chart at the foot of a bed. Nobody needs to be told what 62 in an amber ring means.

  • Drag a spec onto a card — assigns it to that room; the arc recalculates visibly
  • No context menus. Actions live inside the room, never on the card

  • Detects a room stalling before the designer notices and quietly reorders the grid
  • Suggests which room to work on next based on install-day pressure, never on due dates
03

Object Journey

Built

Every piece has a life. Objects travel nine stages — inspiration to maintenance — and the entire project converges on one install day.

Layout logic

Nine stage columns at 118px pitch from x392. Each object is a 62px row: a hairline trail behind it, ghost ticks ahead, and a 32×32 photographic chip at its current position. The chip is the object — you read the room by scanning a vertical of thumbnails.

This is emphatically not a Gantt: nothing encodes duration, only position in a lifecycle.

Navigation philosophy

Left-to-right is the only axis of meaning. There is no calendar to scroll. The single vertical ink line — install day — is the horizon everything moves toward, and it never moves.

Why it fits the model

Procurement is the phase where studios lose money and client trust. The industry already tracks a selection state machine in room-tabbed spreadsheets; this makes that machine visible at a glance and gives the expediting ritual a home.

Understood in five seconds

“Everything is moving right, and one thing is red and behind.” Convergence anxiety is the correct emotional read — it's what install day actually feels like.

04

Confidence Engine

Built

Nothing tracks tasks. Everything tracks confidence — client, designer, budget, construction — and AI predicts where it will collapse.

Layout logic

The only dark screen in the set: charcoal #22201C. Four confidence bands flow left to right on 148px baselines, thickness = confidence. Huge Newsreader Light numerals sit in the left margin like instrument readouts. A 260px right rail lists what moved the needle.

Darkness is doing work here: this is the screen you open when you're worried.

Navigation philosophy

Time is the only navigation. Past is solid, future is a dashed ghost, and a single bone NOW line separates them. There is nothing to click into — this is a read-only instrument, and that restraint is the point.

Why it fits the model

Design problems have no stopping rule, so “% complete” is a lie. Confidence is the only honest measure of progress in work that has no done — and it is what every principal is actually tracking in their head.

Understood in five seconds

“Three ribbons are healthy, one is dying.” No numeracy required. The collapsing band is legible from across the studio.

  • Refuses false precision — scores move in units of one, never decimals
05

Ambient Memory

Built

The record writes itself. Nothing is filed, nothing is searched — knowledge surfaces exactly when it is needed, and the human only confirms.

Layout logic

A single 656px column at x392 and almost nothing else. No sidebar, no tabs, no toolbar. A faint archive ribbon at x1200 hints that everything is remembered; a strip of fragments in the left margin proves it. The emptiness is the feature.

Navigation philosophy

There is no navigation. The system decides what you see, and tells you why in an italic line — “because you opened the kitchen”. The only input is at the bottom and it is not a search box: “Ask anything — or just keep working.”

Why it fits the model

The deepest pain in studios is not speed, it is memory — proving what was decided, by whom, and why. Every prior tool solved this by asking humans to file things, which they never do. This one asks nothing.

Understood in five seconds

“It already knows.” The reaction target is relief, not comprehension. Users should feel they have been quietly assisted rather than presented with software.

06

Versioned World

Spec — ready to build

The whole project behaves like a repository, without a single technical word. Options are branches, decisions are merges, the issued set is a tag — and alternative realities can be walked side by side.

Layout logic

A horizontal trunk runs across the vertical centre at y470 — a 2px ink line, the current reality. Branches leave and rejoin as elegant curves above and below, each carrying a small rendered thumbnail at its head. The right 380px is a “compare” panel showing two realities stacked as images with a diff of what changes between them.

Time flows left to right; divergence is vertical. That is the entire grammar.

Navigation philosophy

You navigate by walking the timeline, not by opening files. A scrubber under the trunk moves the whole project through time; branch heads slide into view as they are created. Clicking a branch doesn't “open” it — the canvas becomes that reality, and the trunk greys to show you've stepped off it.

Why it fits the model

Designers explore and abandon constantly, and current tools frame abandonment as failure. Branches make exploration free and shame-free. It also structurally kills the industry's most expensive error class — building from a superseded revision — because there is only ever one HEAD.

Understood in five seconds

“This is the version we're building, and these are the ones we didn't.” The thumbnails carry it: you can see the alternative kitchen without reading a word.

07

Intent Network

Spec — ready to build

Track intentions, not deliverables. “Warm hospitality”, “maximise daylight”, “reduce visual clutter” are first-class objects, and every decision measurably strengthens or weakens them.

Layout logic

A radial constellation, centred at (620,470). Five or six intents sit as large Newsreader Light words on concentric orbits; decisions are small dots positioned by which intents they serve — a decision serving two intents literally sits between them. Gravity is the layout algorithm and also the metaphor.

Left margin 280px holds the intents as a ranked list with strength meters. Nothing else competes.

Navigation philosophy

You navigate by intent, not by artefact. Selecting “maximise daylight” pulls every contributing decision toward it and pushes everything else to the rim. There are no folders because the organising principle is a value, not a location.

Why it fits the model

The primary generator — the organising idea a designer latches onto early — is the most valuable object in a project and the one no software stores. It is what gets defended in value engineering and lost at staff turnover. Making it a tracked object is the most emotionally resonant idea in the whole set.

Understood in five seconds

“These are the things we promised the building would feel like, and this one is being eroded.” The weakening intent is visibly dimmer and its orbit is thinner.

Interaction
  • Hover an intent — contributing dots migrate toward it over 400ms with spring settle; unrelated dots fade to 12%. The layout is alive
  • Hover a dot — its tension lines light and a two-line tooltip names the trade it made
  • Drag an intent up the rail — reprioritises it; the whole field re-settles and strengths recompute
  • Type anywhere — adds an intent. The field makes room. No “+ new” button exists
  • Nothing can be deleted, only retired — retired intents stay on the rim, greyed, as a record of what the project stopped caring about
AI, embedded
  • Extracts candidate intents from the brief and first meeting transcripts — the designer edits rather than authors
  • Scores every new decision against every intent using vision models on the imagery plus text on the rationale
  • Flags erosion patterns: not single violations but repeated drift in one direction
  • Cannot claim taste. Every score is shown as “our reading”, editable in one click, and the edit trains the project's own model

Promise Ledger

Spec — ready to build

Every commitment made by anyone — client, vendor, contractor, the studio itself — is visible, dated and kept. Designed as a book of trust, not a book of accounts.

Layout logic

A ledger page: a single ruled column 880px wide, centred, on paper #FFFFFF with a 40px warm margin band on the left carrying dates in mono. Ruled hairlines every 56px — actual ruling, like a bound book. Two thin columns at the right edge hold “promised” and “kept” marks.

The typographic register is the highest in the set: Newsreader Regular for every promise, because a promise is a sentence.

Navigation philosophy

You read down. Time is vertical, as in a real ledger. Filtering happens by party — six small avatars in the top margin — and selecting one greys every other line rather than hiding it, preserving the sense of a continuous record.

Why it fits the model

“The owner told me on site to go ahead” is unenforceable and expensive. Verbal approvals, quoted lead times, and internal “by Friday” all evaporate. Writing them down is the highest-value, least-glamorous work in a studio — this makes it automatic and, crucially, mutual.

Understood in five seconds

“This is what everyone said they'd do.” It reads like a document, not an app — which is exactly the authority it needs to carry into a dispute.

Vendor reliability

One supplier's history across every studio on the platform: quoted vs actual lead times as paired bars over two years. The network effect made visible — and the reason a studio can't leave.

Support C

The gentle chase

An unkept promise, three days late. AI has drafted a short, warm message. The screen shows the draft in serif at reading size and the relationship history beneath it — because the tone should depend on the relationship.

  • Hover a row — the ruling above and below darkens slightly, isolating the line like a finger on a page. No highlight fill, ever
  • Click the state mark — cycles open → kept → renegotiated with a 180ms ink draw animation. Marking something kept should feel like signing
  • Click a promise — the row expands in place to show its evidence: the message, the transcript excerpt, the email. The page grows; nothing navigates away
  • Select a party — others fade to 25% and the trust bars re-scale. The record stays whole
  • Promises cannot be deleted. A wrong promise is withdrawn, which leaves a struck line — the ledger's integrity is the product

  • Extracts commitments from every channel — the core capability, and the one LLMs do best
  • Distinguishes a promise from a musing (“we could probably…” is not a promise) and errs toward not recording
  • Warns before a promise breaks, using the party's own history, not a generic reminder
  • Accumulates cross-studio vendor telemetry — real lead times, real reliability — which no incumbent can replicate
  • Tone guard: drafts chases that match the relationship's warmth. Never sends autonomously

Studio Wall

Spec — ready to build

Replace project management with one enormous evolving wall. Moodboards, drawings, voice notes, samples, pinned conversations — everything exists spatially. No folders, no pages, no navigation.

Layout logic

The screen is edge-to-edge canvas — no panels at all. Content clusters by room, loosely, with generous voids between clusters. Items sit at deliberate angles (−6° to +7°) as if pinned by hand. The only chrome is a 44px translucent bar floating bottom-centre and a zoom indicator bottom-right.

Density is the aesthetic: this is the one screen in the set that is allowed to look full.

Navigation philosophy

There is no navigation. Only pan and zoom — the same two gestures a person uses standing in front of a real wall. Zoom level is hierarchy: at 25% you see rooms as coloured clusters, at 60% you see boards, at 100% you read notes, at 200% you inspect a material's weave.

Why it fits the model

Studios already run on walls: pin, cluster, step back, discuss. Pinned references are the substrate of design thinking, not attachments to a task. This is the concept with the highest adoption ceiling and the lowest learning curve — and, on its own, no accountability, which is why it is the surface layer of the recommended composition rather than the whole product.

Understood in five seconds

“That's my wall.” Recognition, not comprehension. A designer should feel their studio has been photographed.

Uncertainty Map

Spec — ready to build

The project seen as weather. The brighter an area, the more unknowns remain — budget, technical, supplier, client, construction — and AI forecasts where the storm arrives weeks before it does.

Layout logic

A single full-bleed topographic field occupying x0–1080, with the floor plan drawn as thin ink linework underneath a field of soft concentric isobars. Areas of high uncertainty glow warm and their contours crowd together; resolved areas are flat and cool. A 360px right column holds the forecast as a written bulletin.

The visual reference is a barometric chart, not a heat map — contour lines, not blocky gradients.

Navigation philosophy

Navigate by where the weather is. There is no list of issues; you click into a pressure system. A five-step time slider at the bottom moves between “now” and “+6 weeks”, and the map animates its forecast — the only screen in the set where the primary interaction is watching.

Why it fits the model

Prioritisation in design is not “what's next” but “what's riskiest and least known”. RFIs, sample requests, unconfirmed dimensions and unreliable lead times are all the same object: an unknown with a blast radius. Sizing them spatially is honest about wickedness in a way a backlog never is.

Understood in five seconds

“There's a storm over the kitchen.” Universally legible — everyone has read a weather map — and it produces the right instinct: go to the weather, don't work down a list.

Support A

Inside a system

The kitchen storm opened: its unknowns listed as serif sentences — “Is the slab wide enough for a single-piece island?” — each with its blast radius and the one action that would collapse it. Questions, not tasks.

Support B

Six weeks out

The slider at maximum: two systems have dissipated, one has merged with another and grown severe. A single serif line names the merge. Forecasting as a design act.

Support C

Historical accuracy

A quiet trust screen: past forecasts against what actually happened, as paired contour thumbnails. If the product predicts, it must be auditable — this is what earns the right to be believed.

Interaction
  • Hover a system — contours pulse outward once (600ms) and leader lines draw to what it threatens
  • Drag the time slider — contours morph continuously; systems visibly form, deepen, merge and dissipate. This is the product's memorable moment
  • Click a contour ring — filters to unknowns at that severity, as sentences, in place
  • Resolving an unknown — the innermost contour dissolves and the rings relax outward over 800ms. Progress you can watch, which no backlog burn-down ever achieves
  • No create button. Unknowns are detected, not entered — if you had to type it, you already knew it
AI, embedded
  • Finds the unknowns — unanswered questions in threads, unconfirmed dimensions, quotes past validity, vendors with slipping history
  • Sizes blast radius by walking the decision graph, which is why this concept needs concept 01 underneath it
  • Forecasts formation: systems appear on the map before anyone has articulated the problem
  • Writes the bulletin in plain sentences and is scored publicly on its accuracy
  • Never assigns work. It reports weather; the studio decides where to sail
